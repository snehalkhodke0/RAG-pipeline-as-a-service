# A Brief History of Computing

## Early Mechanical Computers

Charles Babbage designed the Analytical Engine in 1837, a mechanical general-purpose
computer that was never completed in his lifetime. Ada Lovelace wrote what is
considered the first algorithm intended to be processed by a machine, published in
1843 as notes on the Analytical Engine, which is why she is often called the first
computer programmer.

## The First Electronic Computers

The Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, known as ENIAC, was completed in
1945 at the University of Pennsylvania. ENIAC weighed about 30 tons and used
approximately 17,468 vacuum tubes. It was originally built to calculate artillery
firing tables for the United States Army.

## The Transistor

The transistor was invented in 1947 at Bell Labs by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain,
and William Shockley. The transistor replaced the bulky and unreliable vacuum tube,
enabling smaller, faster, and more reliable computers. The three inventors shared the
1956 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work.

## The Integrated Circuit

Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments demonstrated the first working integrated circuit in
1958. Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor independently developed a similar
integrated circuit shortly afterward. The integrated circuit allowed many transistors
to be placed on a single chip, a foundation of modern computing.

## The Personal Computer Era

The Altair 8800, released in 1975, is widely considered the spark that ignited the
personal computer revolution. The Apple II, released in 1977 by Apple Computer, was
one of the first highly successful mass-produced personal computers. The IBM PC,
introduced in 1981, established many hardware standards still referenced today.

## The Rise of the Internet

The ARPANET, developed by the United States Department of Defense, sent its first
message between two computers in 1969. Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in
1989 while working at CERN, introducing the concepts of URLs, HTML, and HTTP that
still underpin the web today.

## Moore's Law

In 1965, Intel co-founder Gordon Moore observed that the number of transistors on an
integrated circuit doubled approximately every two years. This observation, later
named Moore's Law, has driven the semiconductor industry's pace of innovation for
decades.
